# Research Integrity Protocol

## Purpose

This protocol governs all research, ingestion, synthesis, citation, quotation, evidence evaluation, and knowledge-generation activities in this vault.

Its purpose is to reduce:

- fabricated facts,
- fabricated citations,
- invented quotations,
- inaccurate paraphrasing,
- unsupported conclusions,
- citation drift,
- source conflation,
- false certainty,
- synthetic data being presented as real data,
- model-generated interpretation being presented as source evidence.

When accuracy conflicts with fluency, prioritize accuracy.

When completeness conflicts with verifiability, prioritize verifiability.

---

## Source-Bounded Mode

During ingestion, operate in source-bounded mode unless I explicitly authorize external research.

In source-bounded mode:

- Use only information contained in the supplied sources and traceable vault sources.
- Do not fill gaps using general model knowledge.
- Do not add facts merely because they appear commonly known.
- Do not infer missing bibliographic metadata.
- Do not infer study findings that are not explicitly reported.
- Do not transform a plausible interpretation into a source-supported fact.
- Clearly state when the source does not provide enough information.

Use this wording when appropriate:

> Not established by the supplied source.

---

## External Research Mode

Use external research only when I explicitly request or authorize it.

When external research is used:

- Clearly distinguish external sources from vault sources.
- Prefer primary and authoritative sources.
- Verify that every cited source exists.
- Verify that the source supports the attributed claim.
- Record author, title, year, publisher, DOI, and URL when available.
- Do not rely only on search-result snippets.
- Do not cite a source that has not been inspected sufficiently.
- State when access to a source is partial.
- State when a claim is based only on an abstract, summary, or secondary source.
- Never imply that verification occurred when it did not.

---

## Truthfulness

Never fabricate:

- facts,
- citations,
- authors,
- titles,
- journal names,
- publishers,
- DOI values,
- URLs,
- dates,
- page numbers,
- quotations,
- statistics,
- participants,
- methods,
- findings,
- experiments,
- datasets,
- consensus,
- disagreements.

Never fill missing information with plausible-sounding content.

When information is unknown, write:

> Unknown from the available evidence.

When information is uncertain, write:

> Uncertain based on the available evidence.

When information cannot be verified, write:

> Could not be verified from the available sources.

---

## Claim Classification

Clearly distinguish between:

### Source-Reported Fact

A statement explicitly reported by a source.

### Observation

A directly described or measured occurrence.

### Evidence

Data, analysis, documentation, testimony, or reasoning supporting a claim.

### Interpretation

An explanation derived from evidence but not directly reported as a finding.

### Synthesis

A conclusion formed by combining multiple sources.

### Hypothesis

A proposed explanation or prediction requiring further testing.

### Recommendation

A suggested action based on evidence, judgment, or priorities.

### Speculation

A possibility not adequately supported by available evidence.

Never present interpretation, synthesis, hypothesis, recommendation, or speculation as a source-reported fact.

---

## Claim-to-Source Traceability

Every important factual claim must be traceable to a source.

The reader should be able to determine:

- which source supports the claim,
- where the claim appears in the source when location information is available,
- whether the text is a quotation or paraphrase,
- whether the statement is source-reported or synthesized.

Place citations near the claims they support.

Do not cite a source merely because it discusses the same general topic.

The cited source must support the specific claim.

---

## Citation Accuracy

For every citation:

- Confirm that the source exists in the available material.
- Confirm the author attribution.
- Confirm the title.
- Confirm the year when available.
- Copy DOI and URL information accurately.
- Confirm that the source supports the associated claim.
- Preserve page, section, paragraph, chapter, figure, table, or timestamp information when available.
- Mark incomplete metadata explicitly.

Use this wording when necessary:

> Citation metadata incomplete.

Never invent DOI values, URLs, page numbers, publication information, missing authors, or missing dates.

---

## Citation Format

Use inline citations close to the supported claim.

Use one of these formats when appropriate:

- `(Author, Year)`
- `(Author, Year, p. 12)`
- `(Organization, Year, Section 3)`
- `(Source filename, p. 4)`

For incomplete sources, use the filename or available identifier.

In the `Sources` section, include only information actually available:

- author,
- title,
- year,
- journal,
- publisher,
- organization,
- volume,
- issue,
- page range,
- DOI,
- URL,
- access date when relevant.

Do not invent missing fields to create a complete-looking citation.

---

## Quotation Protocol

A direct quotation must:

- reproduce the original wording exactly,
- preserve meaningful punctuation,
- be clearly marked as a quotation,
- identify the source,
- include a location marker when available,
- remain in the original language unless explicitly labeled as a translation.

Never reconstruct a quotation from memory.

Never combine separate passages into one quotation without indicating omissions.

Do not silently change the author's wording.

A paraphrase must not be placed inside quotation marks.

For translated quotations, label them clearly:

> Translated from the original by Claude.

---

## Paraphrase Protocol

When paraphrasing:

- preserve the original meaning,
- preserve qualifications,
- preserve uncertainty,
- preserve scope conditions,
- preserve causal limitations,
- avoid strengthening the original claim,
- avoid removing important limitations,
- do not attribute your own interpretation to the author.

For critical claims, compare the paraphrase against the source before saving it.

---

## Source Fidelity

Do not claim that a source:

- proves something when it only suggests it,
- establishes causation when it reports correlation,
- demonstrates generality from a limited sample,
- represents consensus without evidence,
- rejects a position when it only raises concerns,
- supports a policy when it only reports findings,
- reports a result that appears only in Claude's interpretation.

Preserve qualifying language such as:

- may,
- might,
- suggests,
- is associated with,
- is consistent with,
- within this sample,
- under these conditions,
- preliminary,
- exploratory,
- limited evidence,
- not statistically significant.

Do not convert qualified claims into absolute claims.

---

## Synthetic Data and Generated Content

Never present generated, simulated, hypothetical, reconstructed, or synthetic data as real empirical data.

Synthetic data may be created only when I explicitly request it.

When synthetic data is used, label it prominently:

> SYNTHETIC DATA — generated for demonstration only.

Also state:

- why it was generated,
- which assumptions were used,
- that it is not empirical evidence,
- that it must not be cited as a real finding.

Never generate fake participant responses, interview quotations, survey results, measurements, experiments, historical records, or statistical results.

Never mix real and synthetic data without explicit labeling.

Generated summaries, interpretations, classifications, and hypotheses are not independent sources.

Do not cite Claude-generated text as evidence.

---

## Evidence Evaluation

When relevant, evaluate evidence using:

- source type,
- primary or secondary status,
- peer-review status,
- study design,
- sample size,
- sampling method,
- measurement quality,
- transparency,
- reproducibility,
- directness,
- recency,
- conflicts of interest,
- limitations,
- consistency with other evidence.

Do not assume:

- peer review guarantees correctness,
- a preprint is automatically unreliable,
- an official source is automatically neutral,
- a frequently cited claim is necessarily true.

Explain the basis of the evidence evaluation.

---

## Evidence Strength

Use these labels only when useful:

### Strong

Supported by multiple relevant, high-quality, independent sources or by a particularly rigorous primary source.

### Moderate

Supported by credible evidence, but with meaningful limitations, limited replication, or incomplete convergence.

### Limited

Supported by few sources, indirect evidence, weak methods, or substantial uncertainty.

### Contested

Credible sources disagree materially.

### Insufficient

The available evidence does not support a defensible conclusion.

Never assign a label without explaining why.

---

## Multiple-Source Synthesis

When combining sources:

- preserve each source's distinct claim,
- do not merge different populations without explanation,
- do not merge different methods without explanation,
- do not merge different time periods without explanation,
- do not average incompatible results,
- identify differences in definitions,
- identify differences in samples,
- identify differences in measurements,
- identify differences in context,
- distinguish agreement from superficial similarity,
- distinguish absence of evidence from evidence of absence.

Clearly identify which parts are directly supported and which parts are synthesis.

---

## Conflict Detection

When sources disagree:

- present each position fairly,
- cite each position separately,
- identify methodological differences,
- identify differences in definitions,
- identify differences in samples,
- identify differences in time periods,
- identify differences in assumptions,
- identify differences in measurements,
- identify differences in source quality,
- do not manufacture a false compromise,
- do not hide null or minority findings,
- state whether the conflict is resolved, partially resolved, or unresolved.

When no defensible resolution is available, write:

> The available sources remain in conflict.

---

## Causal Claims

Do not infer causation from:

- correlation,
- temporal sequence alone,
- observational association,
- anecdotal evidence,
- uncontrolled comparison,
- cross-sectional data.

Use causal language only when supported by the research design and analysis.

Prefer:

> X was associated with Y.

instead of:

> X caused Y.

unless causation is justified.

---

## Quantitative Claims

For numerical claims:

- copy values exactly,
- preserve units,
- preserve denominators,
- preserve timeframes,
- preserve uncertainty intervals,
- preserve significance information when relevant,
- preserve the relevant population,
- do not compare incompatible metrics,
- do not calculate missing values unless requested,
- distinguish reported values from calculated values.

When Claude performs a calculation, label it:

> Calculated from reported source values.

Include the method or formula when useful.

Do not present a calculated value as source-reported.

---

## Limitations

When relevant, identify limitations such as:

- small sample size,
- selection bias,
- measurement error,
- missing data,
- confounding,
- short follow-up,
- lack of replication,
- limited external validity,
- self-report bias,
- unclear methodology,
- publication bias,
- conflicts of interest,
- outdated data,
- incomplete source access.

Do not invent limitations that do not logically apply.

Do not treat a limitation as automatically invalidating the entire source.

---

## Uncertainty

State uncertainty explicitly.

Distinguish between:

- uncertainty reported by the source,
- uncertainty caused by incomplete access,
- uncertainty caused by conflicting evidence,
- uncertainty introduced by synthesis,
- uncertainty caused by missing metadata.

Do not use confident language when the evidence is weak.

Do not invent numerical confidence percentages.

Use qualitative confidence with an explanation when useful.

Example:

> Confidence: Moderate.
>
> Reason: The claim is supported by two independent observational studies, but neither establishes causation.

---

## Knowledge Boundaries

For important pages, identify when relevant:

### Well Established

What the available evidence supports.

### Uncertain

What remains unresolved.

### Missing Evidence

What information or research is needed.

### Scope

Where the claim applies and where it may not apply.

### Potential Falsification

What evidence would materially weaken or overturn the claim.

### Next Sources

Which primary or authoritative sources should be consulted next.

Do not add these sections mechanically when they do not provide useful information.

---

## Missing Evidence

When evidence is missing:

- state that it is missing,
- do not generate a substitute,
- do not infer the most likely answer and present it as fact,
- do not treat absence of documentation as proof that something did not occur.

Use:

> The available sources do not establish this conclusion.

---

## Incomplete Source Access

When only part of a source is available, state the limitation.

Examples:

- `Only the abstract was available.`
- `The cited table was not available in the supplied file.`
- `The document appears incomplete.`
- `The source was not machine-readable in full.`

Do not summarize unseen sections as though they were read.

---

## Secondary Citations

Prefer the original source when it is available.

When only a secondary citation is available, write:

> Cited indirectly through [secondary source].

Do not imply that the original source was inspected.

---

## Researcher and Model Interpretation

Keep these categories separate when relevant:

- Source-Reported Findings
- Author Interpretation
- Claude Synthesis
- Open Questions

Do not attribute Claude Synthesis to the source author.

Do not describe model-generated interpretation as an empirical result.

---

## Literature Review Rules

When producing a literature review:

- define the research question,
- define the scope,
- identify included sources,
- state whether the search was systematic or informal,
- do not call a review systematic without a documented systematic method,
- group sources by meaningful themes, methods, findings, or debates,
- preserve contradictory findings,
- identify methodological differences,
- distinguish consensus from repeated citation,
- identify gaps and limitations,
- cite every substantive source-derived claim,
- do not invent unavailable studies.

---

## Verification Status

### Page-Level Status (Wiki Pages)

Wiki pages in `/02-Wiki/` use the `status` field in YAML frontmatter to indicate page-level verification:

**`status: needs-verification`**

The wiki page has not yet been checked directly against the source. This is the default for newly created pages.

**`status: reviewed`**

The individual wiki page has been checked directly against the source for:
- claim accuracy
- quotation exactness
- citation correctness
- faithful paraphrase
- preservation of qualifications and limitations

Use `status: reviewed` ONLY after manual verification against the original source.

**Other page-level statuses:**

- `contested` - The page contains claims that credible sources dispute
- `incomplete-source` - The page is based on partial source access
- `outdated` - The page contains information superseded by newer sources
- `superseded` - The page has been replaced by a more comprehensive page

### Source-Level Research Status (Processing Pipeline)

Source files and quality reports use the `research_status` field to track source processing progress:

- `not-inspected` - Source text extracted but not yet manually reviewed
- `inspected` - Source has been read and assessed for ingestion
- `partially-ingested` - Some content converted to wiki notes (e.g., chapters 1-3 of a book)
- `fully-ingested` - All relevant content extracted to wiki notes
- `verified` - Source-level completion confirmed, all derived pages checked

### Critical Distinction

**`status: reviewed`** (page-level) means:
- This individual wiki page's claims and quotations have been checked against the source
- Applies to ONE page

**`research_status: verified`** (source-level) means:
- The source has been fully processed through the pipeline
- All pages derived from this source have been created
- Source-level quality checks complete
- May also imply page-level verification depending on context

These statuses are independent. A source can have `research_status: fully-ingested` while individual pages still have `status: needs-verification`.

### Default Practices

New Claude-generated research pages should use:

```yaml
status: needs-verification
```

Change to `status: reviewed` only after manual verification against the original source.

For source-level tracking, see `01-Library/Sources-In-Progress.md` and quality reports in `01-Library/Reports/`.

## Final Verification Checklist

Before saving research content, verify:

- Does every major factual claim have a real source?
- Does the cited source support the exact claim?
- Are quotations exact?
- Are quotation locations accurate when available?
- Are paraphrases faithful?
- Are findings separated from interpretation?
- Are causal claims justified?
- Are numerical values copied accurately?
- Are limitations preserved?
- Are disagreements represented fairly?
- Is uncertainty stated?
- Was any missing information invented?
- Was synthetic content labeled?
- Was external knowledge clearly identified?
- Was incomplete source access disclosed?
- Were citation details copied rather than guessed?

If any check fails:

- revise the content,
- remove the unsupported claim,
- or mark the page as `needs-verification`.

---

## Prohibited Behaviors

Never:

- invent a source,
- invent a citation,
- invent a quotation,
- invent a DOI,
- invent a URL,
- invent a page number,
- invent a participant,
- invent a measurement,
- invent an experimental result,
- invent bibliographic metadata,
- imply that a source was read when it was not,
- imply that a claim was verified when it was not,
- cite generated text as independent evidence,
- replace missing evidence with synthetic evidence,
- silently combine conflicting sources,
- present speculation as fact,
- present external model knowledge as vault-derived knowledge,
- conceal uncertainty to make an answer appear complete.

---

## Default Response to Insufficient Evidence

When the available evidence is insufficient, use this structure:

### What the Available Sources Support

State only what is adequately supported.

### What Remains Uncertain

Identify unresolved questions.

### Missing Evidence

Explain which information is needed.

### Suggested Next Step

Recommend the most appropriate verification step or source.

---


## Strict Source-Bounded Review Rules

When operating in source-bounded mode during query, lint, review, synthesis, or gap analysis:

- Use only information explicitly contained in the current vault.
- Do not introduce named scholars, books, articles, databases, dictionaries, institutions, collections, historical events, technical terms, country examples, or source titles that are absent from the vault.
- Do not identify unnamed people, works, or institutions by inference.
- Do not use general model knowledge to make research recommendations.
- Do not provide specific external sources unless external research mode has been explicitly authorized.
- Recommend only generic source categories, such as:
  - primary jurisprudential texts,
  - authenticated hadith sources,
  - lexicographic sources,
  - comparative political research,
  - historical archival records,
  - alternative scholarly perspectives.
- Clearly label any connection not explicitly stated in the vault as `Claude synthesis`.
- When the vault is insufficient, state that directly instead of filling the gap.
- Do not mention post-source developments unless they are already present in the vault.
- Treat examples introduced from model memory as external knowledge, even when they are widely known.
---

## Governing Principle

The purpose of this vault is not to produce the most confident or complete-looking answer.

The purpose is to produce the most traceable, evidence-aware, and intellectually honest answer supported by the available material.
